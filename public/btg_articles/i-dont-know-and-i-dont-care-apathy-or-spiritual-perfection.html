In ordinary life, phrases like “I don’t know” and “I don’t care” often reflect disengagement, avoidance of responsibility, or emotional indifference. They can weaken relationships, reduce trust, and diminish one’s influence in family, society, and the workplace.


  • ·Silence has consequences: Saying “I don’t know” or “I don’t care” may avoid discomfort today, but it often creates bigger problems later.

  • You lose influence: People stop trusting your judgment, stop seeking your opinion, and begin making decisions without you.

  • Relationships weaken: Partners, friends, and colleagues feel unsupported, leading to emotional distance and weaker teamwork.

  • Growth suffers: Habitual disengagement reduces responsibility, confidence, and leadership opportunities.


From a dharmic perspective, such statements generally indicate a reluctance to participate in one’s prescribed duties (svadharma).


Yet, there exists a profoundly different use of these same sentiments—one that belongs not to apathy but to spiritual perfection.


A striking example is found in King Parikshit. After being cursed to die within seven days from the bite of a serpent, he did not ask who was responsible, seek revenge, or attempt to prolong his life. Instead, he declared: “Let the snake-bird—or whatever magical thing the brahmana created—bite me at once. I only desire that you all continue singing the deeds of Lord Vishnu or Krishna.”


At first glance, this may resemble indifference toward life itself. In reality, it is the highest expression of surrender (saranagati). King Parikshit was not careless; he was completely absorbed in a higher purpose. Having taken shelter of the Lord’s lotus feet, he no longer identified with the temporary body but with his eternal relationship with the Supreme.


The Srimad-Bhagavatam explains that one who has fully surrendered to the Lord becomes fearless in the face of death. Fear arises from attachment to temporary possessions, status, and bodily existence. When these attachments dissolve through devotion, death is no longer perceived as a catastrophe but as a transition toward the Lord.


Thus, the external words may sound similar, but the inner consciousness is worlds apart. An ordinary person’s “I don’t care” often means, “I refuse to engage.” A realized soul’s “I don’t care” means, “Nothing material can distract me from my devotion to Sri Krishna, the Supreme Personality of Godhead.”


The lesson is clear. Before transcendence, indifference is a weakness; after transcendence, detachment becomes a virtue. We should never imitate the outward behaviour of exalted devotees like King Parikshit. Rather, we should emulate the inner quality that made such detachment possible: complete surrender to the Supreme Lord Sri Krishna and unwavering absorption in hearing and glorifying His divine pastimes in the association of pure devotees.—Syamananda Dasa
