What football players can teach

us about Vaishnava relationships


By Vraja Bihari Dasa


In a tense World Cup match that would decide Norway’s future in the tournament, England defeated Norway in a thrilling contest. As the final whistle blew, most expected England’s players to erupt in celebration.


But Jude Bellingham did something unexpected.


Instead of running towards his teammates, he ran towards Erling Haaland. He embraced his disappointed friend and quietly consoled him.


The two were close friends long before they became international rivals. As a young player at Borussia Dortmund, Bellingham had often spoken of the kindness and encouragement he received from Haaland. That friendship remained far deeper than ninety minutes of football. They had even promised each other that if they ever met on opposite sides in an international match, they would not celebrate in front of one another. Their friendship mattered more than the scoreboard.


Bellingham experienced two emotions at the same time. He was delighted that England had won, yet saddened to see his friend heartbroken. Haaland also carried two emotions. He was devastated by Norway’s defeat, yet genuinely happy that his friend had succeeded.


That beautiful paradox is one of the highest expressions of love.


Modern sport often glorifies rivalry, arrogance, and humiliation of the opponent. Yet every now and then, someone reminds us that victory does not require us to stop being human.



Krishna consciousness teaches the same principle.


A devotee does not measure life by whether succeeded or you failed. A devotee rejoices when another devotee advances in Krishna’s service. The success of another devotee is not my loss; it is Krishna’s gain.


The Chaitanya-charitamrita is filled with devotees glorifying one another. They compete—not to become greater—but to appreciate one another more deeply. They delight in another devotee’s service because they know that every act of sincere devotion gives pleasure to Krishna.


In Kali-yuga, it is not difficult to find talented people. What is rare is freedom from envy. To genuinely celebrate another devotee’s success, to appreciate their service without comparison, and to feel joy when Krishna is pleased through them—these qualities are precious ornaments of a Vaishnava.


The friendship between Haaland and Bellingham reminds us to see beyond temporary situations. Today’s victory may become tomorrow’s defeat. Managements change, positions change, circumstances change. But Vaishnava relationships are eternal because every devotee is eternally dear to Krishna.


Our real success is not that we stand above another devotee. Our success is that we stand beside them.


The scriptures describe a Vaishnava as para-duhkha-duhkhi—one who feels another’s suffering as his own. But he is also para-sukha-sukhi—one who delights in another’s happiness as if it were his own.


When another devotee succeeds and we sincerely rejoice, Krishna notices something far greater than the external achievement. He sees a heart becoming free from envy. And that heart is most attractive to Him.


The World Cup lasts a few weeks. Vaishnava relationships last forever. Choose carefully which victory matters more.

“You are one of us”


When correction should never become rejection


When Norway’s World Cup hopes came to an end, emotions ran high. Erling Haaland was visibly frustrated when Alexander Sørloth failed to pass him the ball during a crucial attack. Many fans concluded that this single moment had cost Norway its chance of progressing further. In the days that followed, Sørloth reportedly received hundreds of thousands of hateful messages. Overnight, he became the villain.


Yet something remarkable happened later.


When the Norwegian team returned home after reaching the quarterfinals, they were greeted as heroes. The King of Norway welcomed them, crowds gathered to celebrate them, and the nation expressed its gratitude. No one threw eggs at Sørloth. No one demanded that he be excluded from the team. Instead, one banner beautifully captured the spirit of the occasion: “Alexander Sørloth, you are one of us.”


That is how cultured people treat their own. They may not like what you did, but they don’t stop loving who you are.


In every family, every team, and every spiritual community, people will make mistakes. Sometimes those mistakes have painful consequences. We may feel disappointed, hurt, or even angry. But if every mistake leads to rejection, condemnation, and alienation, we gradually destroy the very relationships that hold the community together.


The same principle applies in Krishna consciousness. Every devotee is struggling with material nature. We all have our moments when we fail Krishna, fail others and even fail ourselves. Devotees will sometimes speak unwisely, make poor decisions, or fail in their responsibilities. If our first instinct is to judge harshly, label them, or push them away, we create a culture of fear rather than a culture of devotion.


Acceptance does not mean approving of every action. Nor does it mean abandoning standards or accountability. It means remembering that behind every mistake is a soul sincerely trying to return to Krishna. We may correct the behaviour, but we should not reject the person.


We are a hospital; not police station


There is another way to look at our spiritual communities. They are more like hospitals than police stations/courtrooms. We have all come to Krishna with the disease of material consciousness. Every devotee is trying to overcome anarthas, learn to love Krishna, and become a better servant. Some recover quickly, while others struggle for years.


So, when we see a devotee stumble, we should remember that we are looking at a patient, not a criminal. Patients need treatment, encouragement, and time to recover. Our temples should resemble hospitals more than courtrooms, and we are all at different stages of recovery. If we turn our temples into courtrooms where everyone is investigated, judged, and condemned, many wounded devotees will simply stop coming. Krishna consciousness is meant to be a place where people heal, not a place where they are written off.


The question is not whether devotees will make mistakes. They will. The real question is: when they do, will they find judges or family? A healthy spiritual community is one in which devotees know that even after they fail, they will still be met with compassion, guidance, and the reassuring message: “You are one of us.”


That spirit reflects Krishna’s own dealings with His devotees. He corrects them, purifies them, and guides them—but He never stops accepting them as His own. When we learn to deal with one another in the same way, our communities become places where devotees can grow, heal, and deepen their relationship with Krishna together.


Krishna consciousness is meant to be a family.
