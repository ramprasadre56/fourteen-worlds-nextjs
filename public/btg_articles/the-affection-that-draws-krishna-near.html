There are certain evenings in spiritual life
when devotion feels unexpectedly gentle.


By Charu Bali


The arati has ended, the temple room slowly grows quiet, and the fragrance of incense still lingers softly in the air. A devotee remains seated for a few extra moments before the Deities, not asking for anything in particular, not even speaking very much internally, but simply reluctant to leave. In such moments, Krishna does not feel distant or philosophical. He feels near.


Yet spiritual life does not always unfold with such sweetness.


There are also days when chanting feels distracted, when the mind wanders endlessly despite sincere effort, and when one’s practices continue more from discipline than inspiration. A devotee may still rise early, attend arati, complete rounds, and carry on with service, while quietly wondering within the heart whether genuine love for Krishna has truly awakened at all.


Such moments can feel deeply vulnerable because they reveal an important truth about bhakti: devotion is not merely a matter of external practice or emotional intensity. Very often, it grows quietly beneath the surface, almost unnoticed, through small acts of remembrance and affection repeated over time.


In Bhagavad-gita (4.11), Krishna says:


ye yatha mam prapadyante
tams tathaiva bhajamy aham


“As all surrender unto Me, I reward them accordingly.”


Krishna reciprocates personally with the mood in which one approaches Him. Where there is sincerity, He gives guidance. Where there is dependence, He gives protection. And where there is affection, He reveals something of His own heart.


This is one of the most beautiful mysteries within bhakti. The Supreme Lord, who sustains innumerable universes and remains complete in Himself, willingly allows Himself to become conquered by love.


Thus, in Srimad-Bhagavatam (9.4.63), the Lord tells Durvasa Muni:


aham bhakta-paradhino
hy asvatantra iva dvija


“I am dependent upon My devotees. I appear to have no independence.”


The statement is astonishing in its tenderness. Krishna, whom no material power can control, accepts the gentle authority of affection.


Just as a flower naturally turns toward sunlight, the heart of the Lord turns toward even the smallest sincere expression of love from His devotee.


That affection often reveals itself not through extraordinary spiritual achievements, but through deeply ordinary moments of remembrance. A devotee instinctively folds their hands upon hearing the holy name. Someone pauses before a meal to first offer it to Krishna. Another softly speaks to the Lord within the mind while walking through the difficulties of an ordinary day. Externally, such actions may appear simple, almost insignificant, yet they gradually transform spiritual practice from ritual into relationship.


Over time, it is possible for spiritual life to become centered primarily around discipline, responsibility, and external practice. Certainly, sadhana is essential, for discipline protects devotion just as riverbanks guide the flow of water. Yet without affection, even sincere practice can gradually begin to feel distant and mechanical.


A lamp may still be offered carefully before the Deities. Mantras may still be recited attentively. Service may continue faithfully each day. But affection brings warmth into these practices. It allows one to remember that Krishna is not merely to be worshiped formally, but lovingly remembered as a person.


One sees this especially in children. A small child carrying a tiny Deity of Lord Jagannatha into an examination hall may understand little philosophy, yet before beginning the paper, the child quietly places it before the Lord and whispers a prayer. In that simple gesture lives the essence of bhakti — not impressive scholarship or perfected meditation, but trust, closeness, and natural affection.


Srila Prabhupada’s own life reflected this same principle. He endured uncertainty, physical hardship, loneliness, rejection, and immense responsibility, yet beneath every struggle remained his deeply affectionate relationship with Krishna. That affection became the hidden strength behind his extraordinary perseverance.


Perhaps this is the deeper inner journey of bhakti — not merely learning the practices of devotion, but gradually learning how to belong to Krishna with simplicity and sincerity of heart.


And when even a small amount of such affection awakens within the heart, Krishna responds in ways often too subtle for the world to notice, yet deeply transformative for the devotee who experiences them.


Like the devotee lingering quietly before the Deities after arati has ended, one slowly discovers that the greatest treasure in spiritual life is not merely the ability to perform devotion, but the grace of developing a loving relationship with the Lord Himself.


For the Infinite, though complete in every way, willingly comes near to a heart that remembers Him with affection.
