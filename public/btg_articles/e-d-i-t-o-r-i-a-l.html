Celebrating Foolishness


In early modern Europe, calendar reforms shifted the New Year from springtime to January, and those who continued celebrating in early April were jokingly called “April Fools.”


Over time, the day evolved into a mass cultural movement, when society temporarily suspends seriousness and allows people a bit of fresh air in the form of good-natured laughter. Yet behind this seemingly light-hearted observance lies a deeper reflection on the nature of wisdom, ignorance, and creativity.


In the modern world, an intriguing reinterpretation of “foolishness” came from Steve Jobs during his famous 2005 commencement address at Stanford University. Jobs ended his speech with the memorable advice:


“Stay hungry, stay foolish.”


At first hearing, the phrase sounds paradoxical. Why would anyone aspire to remain foolish? But Jobs used the term in a very specific sense. For him, foolishness meant the courage to remain curious, to question established patterns, and to attempt what others consider unrealistic. Many of the innovations that transformed modern life emerged from people who were initially dismissed as dreamers or eccentrics.


In this sense, the “fool” is not someone devoid of intelligence, but someone bold enough to venture beyond conventional thinking.


Interestingly, this modern celebration of creative “foolishness” stands alongside a much older and more nuanced discussion of folly in India’s classical wisdom literature. Texts such as the Panchatantra and the Hitopadesa begin by sharply criticizing the condition of the fool. These works, traditionally attributed to the sage Vishnu Sharma, were designed to educate three young princes in the art of responsible governance. The stories repeatedly warn that a fool is not merely someone who lacks knowledge, but someone who refuses to learn. Such a person ignores wise counsel, acts impulsively, and allows arrogance to cloud judgment. Through vivid animal fables and memorable narratives, these texts emphasize that wisdom arises from reflection, humility, and the willingness to learn from experience.


The same perspective is expressed in the celebrated teachings of Vidura in the Mahabharata. In the Vidura-niti, Vidura offers profound advice on ethics, leadership, and self-discipline. He describes the fool as someone who speaks excessively, disregards good advice, and cannot distinguish between beneficial and harmful actions. The wise person, by contrast, listens carefully, reflects deeply, and acts with restraint. In this dharmic framework, foolishness is not an amusing trait but a spiritual and moral limitation that prevents the expansion of wisdom.


How then do we reconcile these two perspectives—the playful foolishness celebrated in April and the stern warnings found in ancient texts? The answer lies in understanding that the word “fool” carries two very different meanings. The first is the destructive kind described in classical literature: the person who refuses to grow, ignores wisdom, and repeats the same mistakes. The second is the creative “foolishness” celebrated by thinkers like Steve Jobs—the courage to question assumptions, explore new ideas, and remain intellectually humble.


When seen together, these perspectives offer a valuable lesson. The purpose of life is not to eliminate risk or experimentation, but to combine curiosity with discernment. One must avoid the stubborn ignorance of the fool while retaining the openness and wonder that allow new insights to emerge. Perhaps that is the deeper message hidden behind the laughter of April Fools’ Day. It reminds us that while human beings are capable of folly, they are also capable of growth. The challenge is not merely to laugh at foolishness, but to transform it into wisdom.


Taking the concept of remaining a fool at the highest level of spirituality is evident from this excerpt from a lecture by Srila Prabhupada: “It is not that because I am acting as guru, I am no longer a student. No, I am still a student. Chaitanya Mahaprabhu taught us this instruction. That we shall always remain a foolish student before our Guru Maharaja. That is Vedic culture. I may be a very big man, but still, I should remain a foolish student to my guru. That is the qualification. Guru more murkha dekhi’ karila sasana [Cc Adi 7.71]. We should always be prepared to be controlled by the guru. That is a very good qualification. Yasya prasadad bhagavat-prasado. . . ara na kariha mane asa. So we should always be obedient students to our guru. That is the qualification. That is the spiritual qualification.” (Lecture, Hyderabad 12/10/76)


— Syamananda Das
