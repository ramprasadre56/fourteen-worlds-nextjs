When Service Becomes a Race… and Krishna Quietly Waits


By Charu Bali


There is a silence that arrives at the close of the day—not announced or invited, but gently descending like dusk upon a restless world. It comes when the last note of activity fades, when hands finally pause, and when even devotion, having moved through its many expressions, rests in stillness.

In that quiet, a life of service appears before the heart—a devotee rising before dawn for mangala-arati, chanting the holy name, moving through the pages of sastra like one searching for home, serving the Deities with loving care, offering time, energy, words, and thought in the spirit of surrender.

Outwardly, the journey appears purposeful, even luminous. Yet another movement quietly begins—not of the body, but of the heart.


“Have I done enough?”

“Could my chanting have been more attentive?”

“Have I lost too much time before coming to this path?”

“Will I ever become what I am meant to become?”

These questions seldom seek an audience, yet they accompany many sincere devotees—not because they doubt Krishna, but because they long to please Him more perfectly.

When Devotion Quietly Becomes a Race


One of the subtle dangers on the path of bhakti is that enthusiasm can slowly become anxiety. Without realizing it, devotional service begins to resemble a race—to chant better, serve more, know more, become more worthy.


Hidden beneath this sincere aspiration lies another thought: “I came too late.”


Many devotees arrive at Krishna consciousness after years spent pursuing worldly ambitions. Instead of rejoicing that Krishna has mercifully called them, they grieve what they imagine were lost years.

“If only I had joined earlier…”


“Now I must make up for everything.”

Each passing day begins to feel like a race against time. Yet this is not the language of divine love; it is more often the language of fear.


The Bhagavad-gita does not begin with Krishna reminding Arjuna how late he is. Standing before a bewildered disciple, Krishna does not dwell on Arjuna’s past. He awakens his future. Perhaps this is how He sees every sincere soul who turns toward Him.

Alongside this urgency, another quiet anxiety often appears: “Am I serving Krishna… or my desire to be appreciated?”

The appreciation of Vaishnavas naturally encourages the heart. Yet the sincere practitioner sometimes begins to question even that encouragement. “Perhaps I simply want recognition.”

Our acaryas describe pratishtha—the desire for honour and recognition—as one of the subtlest anarthas. Yet its presence does not invalidate bhakti; it reveals why bhakti is necessary. After countless lifetimes seeking prestige, why should such tendencies disappear the moment one begins devotional service?

Bhakti does not require a perfect heart before service. Rather, through sincere service, the heart is gradually revealed and purified. We do not offer Krishna our perfection; we offer Him ourselves.

Purification Happens Through Service


A gentle misconception sometimes settles in the heart—that before one can truly serve Krishna, one must first become worthy or pure. Yet the scriptures speak with remarkable tenderness.

Sri Chaitanya Mahaprabhu begins the Sikshashtakam with the assurance, ceto-darpana-marjanam bhava-maha-davagni-nirvapanam: “The holy name cleanses the mirror of the heart.” Purification, then, is not a prerequisite for chanting; it is the gift found within chanting itself.

Krishna offers the same reassurance in the Bhagavad-gita.

Patram pushpam phalam toyam


yo me bhaktya prayacchati:


“If one offers Me with devotion a leaf, a flower, a fruit, or water, I accept it.” (Gita 9.26)


He does not ask us to come after becoming flawless. He simply invites us to offer.


In that invitation, the mood of bhakti is beautifully revealed. We become qualified not before serving, but by serving. As the heart is purified through sincere devotional service, Krishna gradually entrusts the devotee with deeper opportunities to serve Him—not as a reward for personal achievement, but as an expression of His mercy.


The Bhagavad-gita reveals this progression:

mam ca yo ‘vyabhicarena

bhakti-yogena sevate

sa gunan samatityaitan

brahma-bhuyaya kalpate


Steady devotional service gradually lifts one beyond the modes of material nature.


Yet purification is not the destination. Krishna continues:

brahma-bhutah prasannatma

na socati na kankshati

samah sarveshu bhuteshu

mad-bhaktim labhate param


Purity opens the doorway to deeper devotional service.


Finally, Krishna reveals the culmination of this journey:


bhaktya mam abhijanati

yavan yas casmi tattvatah

tato mam tattvato jnatva

visate tad-anantaram


Only through bhakti can one truly know Him and ultimately enter His eternal abode.


Bhakti, therefore, is both the path and the destination. We do not wait outside the path until we become worthy. We step into service as we are, trusting that through that very service Krishna gradually purifies the heart and lovingly draws the devotee ever closer to Him.


The Illusion of Being the Doer

A subtle shift can quietly take place in the heart of a devotee. What begins as sincere service gradually gathers the weight of personal responsibility until it feels as though everything depends upon one’s own effort.

“I must carry this responsibility.”

“I must make this difference.”

Bhakti is never passive, and sincere endeavour is always valued. Yet the Bhagavad-gita gently corrects our inner vision:

prakriteh kriyamanani

gunaih karmani sarvasah

ahankara-vimudhatma

kartaham iti manyate


“The bewildered soul, under false ego, thinks himself the doer.”


Krishna does not deny activity; He simply shifts our understanding of authorship. The same hands may serve, the same intelligence may plan, but the results always remain in His hands.


Later, He offers Arjuna another intimate instruction, nimitta-matram bhava savya-sacin (Gita 11.33): “Become merely an instrument.”


This single instruction quietly lifts a burden many devotees carry. We are neither the centre of the mission nor the bearers of its success. We are simply instruments, serving under the shelter of guru and Krishna.


Our responsibility is sincerity in service. The effort belongs to us. The outcome belongs to Him.

The Measure of Success


The world measures success through visibility, recognition, and achievement. In bhakti, the measure is entirely different.

Not how much we perform.


Not how many people we influence.


Not how much appreciation we receive.

The question is far simpler:


Is guru pleased?


Is Krishna pleased?

Everything else gradually fades into the background.

Sri Chaitanya Mahaprabhu beautifully describes the inner mood that sustains such devotion:


trinad api sunicena

taror api sahishnuna

amanina manadena

kirtaniyah sada harih


Humility is not weakness or self-negation. It is the atmosphere in which bhakti naturally breathes—where the heart is no longer striving to prove itself, but simply longing to remember and serve.

The Finish Line Krishna Never Drew


Perhaps the deepest irony of devotional life is this: while the heart struggles to become worthy of Krishna’s love, the scriptures quietly reveal that it is Krishna’s love that makes one worthy.


He is not searching for flawless devotees.


He is not waiting for perfected lives.


He is waiting for honesty.


The honesty to stand before Him and simply say,


“I still struggle.”


“I still seek appreciation.”


“I still become anxious.”


“I still carry so many anarthas within me.”


“But still… I want to serve You.”

Such honesty is not a disqualification. It is often the beginning of genuine surrender.

Krishna’s final instruction in the Bhagavad-gita is both complete and deeply reassuring:

sarva-dharman parityajya


mam ekam saranam vraja


“Abandon all varieties of duty and simply surrender unto Me.”


For many practitioners, this surrender also means releasing the burden of constantly measuring one’s spiritual progress or asking whether one is “enough.” Instead, a quiet trust begins to grow—that sincere endeavour, offered under the guidance of guru and Vaishnavas, is never forgotten by Krishna.


Seen in this light, life no longer feels like a race.


There is no invisible deadline.


No competition with others—or even with ourselves.


No finish line waiting to be crossed.


There is only a relationship, quietly deepening through every attentive round, every unnoticed act of service, every sincere prayer, and every humble attempt to please Him.


The world may remember achievement. Krishna remembers sincerity.


And perhaps this is the greatest reassurance for every aspiring devotee: The finish line we were searching for was never drawn by Krishna. He never asked us to become extraordinary.
