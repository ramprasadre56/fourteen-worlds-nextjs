Rediscovering Meaning Through Spiritual Learning


How bhakti-yoga gave education its meaning back


by Akshobhya Tirtha Dasa


I grew up in India at a time when India stood at a quiet crossroads—ancient in memory, restless in aspiration. Around me were schools that rewarded competition, families that spoke the language of achievement, and a shared belief that success was measured by earnings and status. Yet threaded through everyday life was a deeper current: temples nestled into neighborhoods, rituals that slowed time, and stories that reminded us that we are more than what we accumulate or achieve.


Every Sunday morning, while the city still slept, my mother would gather us for what felt like an ordinary ritual—a visit to the Sri Sri Radha Govinda temple on Albert Road in Kolkata. It was the summer of 1979, and the temple building was half-renovated, the air thick with incense and the smell of fresh paint. To many, it looked unfinished. To me, it felt alive. The moment we stepped through the gate, the noise of the world softened into rhythm—the gentle chiming of karatalas, the hum of voices, the swirl of white and saffron robes.


The teachers there were nothing like the ones I knew at school. They smiled easily, spoke gently, and seemed genuinely happy to be in our company. They didn’t demand silence or obedience; they invited curiosity. Through stories of kings, saints, and seekers, they conveyed an incisive but simple truth—that we are not merely bodies moving through the world, but souls journeying through experiences in material bodies. And that the soul’s deepest relationship is not with achievement, wealth accumulation, or worldly titles, but with the Supreme Soul, Sri Krishna.


I remember the life-sized murti of Srila Prabhupada seated in peaceful meditation. In the temple hall, people sang and danced, yet he remained as if in deep absorption — not withdrawn, but radiant. Even as a child, I sensed that meditation was not emptiness; it was fullness in the lived moment. The hall vibrated with devotion under the loving gaze of a much-adored and ever-present spiritual master. Within the heart of that energy, at the temple, I felt deep joy and gratitude.


The temple became my first glimpse of a different kind of education, one that nourished the soul as much as the mind. At school, we learned how to compete; at the temple, we learned how to relate to one another with compassion. At school, we were tested on what we could remember; at the temple, we were reminded of what we had forgotten — our own source of and connection with divinity. This distinction, though subtle, would later become the axis of my life’s work.


And then there was prasadam. After each class, we would sit cross-legged on the floor and receive simple meals collectively prepared and served with care. It wasn’t just food; it was sanctified grace. At school, lunchtime was noisy and hurried, a scramble for the best lunchbox. At the temple, it was an act of reverence—a reminder that nourishment could be sacred. Even as a child, I sensed that love tasted different when it was offered spontaneously, rather than as a motivated exchange of something in return. It was my earliest lesson in bhakti as lived practice—that devotion is not abstract belief, but a sweet relationship with the Lordships that is also expressed through daily acts of loving exchanges among devotees.


These experiences planted a seed that lay dormant for years, the understanding that education could be a spiritual journey. But as I grew older, the noise of the world grew louder. School, university, career, marriage, family responsibilities—the drumbeats of ambition reclaimed me. I became fluent in the language of goals and performance and forgot the melody of wonder. The seed of that early awakening in that unfinished temple hall waited underground, waiting for the right season to germinate.


Years later, when I began working with young people as an education counselor and mentor, I recognized the same restlessness in their eyes that I once carried. These were capable, intelligent students, often outwardly successful, yet inwardly disconnected. They were not lacking ambition; they were lacking anchoring. They had learned how to compete, but not how to listen to themselves. Many felt hollow not because they lacked ambition, but because their ambition lacked direction. What they were missing was not motivation, but connection—to purpose, to service, to Krishna. Many intuitively sensed that they were more than bodies chasing outcomes, yet had no framework to live that truth.



Still, it never disappeared. From time to time, a kind word, a verse of scripture, or the sight of the rising sun would stir something familiar. The temple’s music would echo faintly, like a half-remembered song. It whispered that learning was meant to awaken, not merely to equip.


It took me years to understand that the temple and the classroom were not opposites, but incomplete halves. Education without spirituality creates efficiency without empathy. Spirituality without understanding risks sentiment without structure. What Srila Prabhupada envisioned for India was precisely this integration: a society grounded in spiritual clarity yet capable of thoughtful action, where knowledge served consciousness, and progress did not come at the cost of compassion. India, he believed, was meant not to imitate the West, but to offer the world a deeper way of being through education that formed character and connection with divinity, not just careers and consumerism.


My life’s work, I now understand, is not merely guiding students and young professionals toward universities or opportunities. It is helping them remember who they are beneath achievement. To serve Krishna, for me, is to serve consciousness in action: to mentor with humility, to teach without domination, and to help young people align their talents with contribution rather than comparison. It is to carry forward Srila Prabhupada’s vision in a contemporary language—by shaping spaces where learning awakens responsibility, compassion, and inner steadiness.


I have learned that teaching is not the transfer of information, but the transmission of orientation. The educator’s task is not to fill minds, but to light a lamp—to dissolve the darkness born of ignorance with clarity rooted in love. That lamp is not lit by grades or credentials, but by presence.


As an educator, I realized that inspiration must be accompanied by invitation into practice of bhakti-yoga. Youth do not transform through ideology alone; they transform through small, lived experiences.



I have seen this repeatedly. One student, burdened by anxiety and self-comparison, was gently encouraged to begin each day with just five minutes of chanting—not as obligation, but as meditative grounding. Over months, that simple practice became a refuge. Another student, initially resistant to anything “religious,” began by reading a single verse of the Bhagavad-gita each week, and discussed each verse not academically but as practical applied wisdom. What shifted was not belief, but orientation—from hankering for achievement toward inquiry of a higher purpose in life.


Several students who struggled with entitlement and restlessness found unexpected steadiness through seva—whether by helping peers with the kindness challenges we organize, assisting in the home programs we run for kids, or consciously honoring food as prasadam before eating. These were not dramatic conversions, but beginnings. And that is often how bhakti enters—softly, respectfully, without coercion.


This is the calling of youth preachers today: not to demand devotion, but to model and offer pathways into it—to show young people that chanting is not escapism but centering; that prasadam is not ritual but gratitude; that making compassion a default setting is the way to live a fulfilling life, that studying sacred texts is not dogma but dialogue between the soul and the Supreme Soul Krishna.


The boy who once sat cross-legged in that unfinished temple hall did not know he was sitting at the doorway of his life’s service. Yet those early mornings—the sound of cymbals, the stillness of Srila Prabhupada’s gaze, the sanctity of prasadam—became the unseen foundation of everything I now offer.


Education, I have come to see, is meant to awaken the soul. The first step in that awakening is humility—the recognition that we are not the body, but eternal servants of the Supreme Soul. And the most natural expression of that humility is service: to Krishna, and through Krishna, to others.


Akshobhya Tirtha Dasa (aka Arjun Seth) is the Founder of School for the Heart and Edbrand, Educator based in Gurugram Delhi-NCR, who integrates spiritual wisdom with modern academic mentorship and leadership coaching.
