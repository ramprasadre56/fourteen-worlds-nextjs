Between Expectation and Grace


In an age of instant replies, devotional life confronts a deeper
question: what does it mean when God does not answer immediately?


By Ashutosh Patnaik


In a hyper-interactive world, we are hooked to “instant” everything—instant food, instant music, instant payment and, increasingly, instant access to one another. Over time, this ecosystem of speed has quietly trained our minds to expect the same level of urgency in our dealing with others.


In most areas of life, brief delays remain inconsequential. Yet in communication, even a pause of minutes can trigger discomfort. The mind fills with anxiety if a reply isn’t immediate, and even more hurt if a message is seen but ignored. This raises a deeper question: are these moments of distress the result of genuine interpersonal failure, or are they signs of a technological architecture that has erased the boundary between availability and obligation? As a result of such shift in expectations, countless relationships suffer—not because something is truly wrong, rather someone failed to meet the unworded expectation of “instant” reply.


When prayer is loaded with expectations


In devotional life, this conditioning quietly follows us into our relationship with the God. Our baseline expectation remains the same: instant-communication. We bank upon our prayers, often pouring our hearts into them, hoping for some form of reciprocation from the other side. Yet when that response does not arrive, silence is misread as absence, or delay is mistaken for indifference. It is precisely at this point that faith becomes vulnerable.


In the Vaishnava tradition, divinity doesn’t imply any abstract force, rather it has a personal presence—the Supreme Personality of Godhead—Lord Krishna. The Srimad-Bhagavatam describes how exactly as the Lord appears in his personal form:


barhapidam nata-vara-vapuh karnayoh karnikaram


bibhrad vasah kanaka-kapisam vaijayantim ca malam


Crowned with peacock feathers shimmering in emerald green, blue karnikara flowers grace His ears, adorned with pitambara—yellow silk glowing like molten gold, upon His chest sways the fragrant Vaijayanti garland. Thus adorned, Sri Krishna reveals His beautiful transcendental form.”


This vision makes devotion deeply tangible—and paradoxically, this very intimacy heightens expectation. When God is personal, silence is no longer neutral; it is felt. Such a faith, as beautiful as it is, is also fragile in its early stages. In the initial phase, devotion feels thick and immersive in one moment and suddenly thin and scattered in the next. During such a state, the heart instinctively seeks constant reassurance, which is why we subconsciously expect prompt communication from God—an immediate sign, or a mystical feeling. Such is nature of utsaha-mayi-bhakti—enthusiasm-driven cycle of devotion in which spiritual practice is fueled primarily by emotional inspiration, novelty, and hope for quick spiritual experience, rather than by deep philosophical grounding.


The phase between devotion and diversion


It’s no wonder, social media is full of memes projecting the humor of human devotion: someone chanting on the right, while scrolling on the left, some chanting while sprinting in a hall, and many others who plainly doze off while chanting.


All such relatable day-to-day scenes are often brushed aside as signs of fatigue, overstimulation, or busy lives. Yet beneath the humor there rests a quieter, more discomforting truth: a simmering resentment toward God—a God perceived as silent, passive, indifferent. Over time, this unspoken emotional baggage weighs down the practice of devotion, turning what was sustained as a monologue, occasionally punctuated by moments of absorption, into a mechanical ritual. Such is the experience of devotion in its ghana-tarala phase—sometimes deep, other times shallow.


When such unexamined emotions recur long enough without philosophical anchoring, a further shift can occur. It is then that God is placed on trial in the inner court of the heart—and, without formal words, He is functionally bracketed from daily life. Although the eternal relationship is never severed, during such temporary disconnect the heart does not remain empty for long. It seeks relief, intensity, or reassurance elsewhere.


Few respond by plunging back into hedonism, chasing pleasure and stimulation to fill the vacuum left behind. Others take a subtler route, settling into a “do good, be good” worldview—ethical and functional, yet largely disconnected from God. For majority, the shift is less dramatic and more ordinary. Life simply fills the gap. Work, self-improvement, social causes, and family obligations expand to occupy the available mental space. Such is the state of mind caught in sankalpa–vikalpa—oscillating between devotion and diversion. In such cases, the relationship with the divine is not violently rejected; it is simply replaced or reduced.


Why bhakti needs tattva?


When the heart becomes heavy with mixed emotions—hope, disappointment, longing, resentment—it can no longer be trusted to lead on its own. At such times, it must fall upon the head to guide the heart forward, rather than letting it sink into quiet emptiness. This is precisely why guides on the path of devotion caution that being “lazy” in philosophical study leads to a weak heart that is easily misguided. Hence, the scriptures emphasize grounding devotion in tattva (devotional anchoring), not as dry philosophy but as inner orientation.


Srila Bhaktivinoda Thakura underscores the path to such spiritual anchoring through sravanam (attentive reception of the scriptures), mananam (thoughtful reflection on wisdom), and nididhyasanam (steady assimilation of devotion into daily life), so that devotion does not depend solely on emotional states.


When our understanding of tattva remains raw or half-baked, a subtle fault line appears—the gap between our expectations and our lived experience of devotion. We begin, often unconsciously, to treat bhakti as a claim: a right to experience God on demand. And when answers do not arrive—especially in moments of crisis—frustration quietly sets in.


This unmet expectation, more than doubt or disbelief, lies at the very heart of the faith crisis. Not because the Lord withdraws His grace, but because our framework for receiving it has not yet matured. Srila Jiva Gosvami cautions us against such narrow expectations by zooming on a multi-dimensional and progressive metric as outlined in the Paramatma-sandarbha. The Lord reveals Himself through different manifestations, each serving a distinct purpose and accessible in different ways:


Para-avatara (as direct manifestation)


A direct, face-to-face experience of the Lord is certainly possible — but it is never something one can presume upon or demand. History records many such manifestations, yet they occur under very specific conditions: to rescue an innocent devotee from catastrophic danger, to dismantle a demonic force disrupting cosmic order, or to reciprocate with someone utterly sold out in devotion.


Under ordinary circumstances, the Lord’s direct manifestation is never brought about by human endeavor alone; it unfolds solely by His independent will. Bhakti, therefore, is not a contractual claim upon God, nor a technique to compel revelation. It is a loving approach—one that humbly invites His mercy; wherein the Lord is not drawn by urgency or insistence, but by the depth, sincerity, and selflessness of devotion.


Vyüha / Antaryami-avatara (as indwelling guide)


The next mode of experiencing the Lord is far more immediate and subtle — His presence as antaryami, the indwelling Supersoul. As Krishna declares in the Bhagavad-gita (15.15), He resides in the heart of all beings, guiding remembrance, knowledge, and forgetfulness. In this sense, the voice of conscience is voice of the Divine.


Many experience this guidance while navigating life’s moral and existential crossroads. Yet the challenge is not the absence of that voice, but the noise that crowds it out. The restless mind routinely drowns out conscience, making divine guidance difficult to discern.


To fully perceive the inner voice requires deep meditation on the Lord as the indwelling spirit— untouched by the dualities of life. But such perception demands steadiness of mind. If you have spent any time all alone, you know this pattern well. You sit with the intention of simply observing as open awareness, witnessing experience as it is, and within seconds, you are effectively hijacked! Some random event of the past—an argument you picked up with your spouse, or a point you forgot to put in your deck or the wall you’re starring at seems overdue for a fresh coat of paint—clouds your awareness. Thus, while the experience of antaryami is profound and real, it’s less practical as a daily mode of spiritual connection.


Nama-avatara (as the holy name)


The Lord ever accessible, appears through his Nama-avatara (holy name). The Sri Caitanya-caritamrita declares:


kali-kale nama-rüpe
krishna-avatara


nama haite haya
sarva-jagat-nistara


“In the age of Kali, the Lord descends in the form of His holy name, opening a sonic portal to deliver the mortal world back to the spiritual abode.” Here, theology is unequivocal: the Lord is non-different from His name. Conceptually, this is clear. Experientially, however, it often feels abstract. So how do we reconcile doctrine with lived experience?


Some attribute this wedge between experience and theory is due to samskaras or karmic conditioning. Yet this explanation is incomplete; if the holy name is non-different from the Lord, no material conditioning can limit His presence. Others point to offenses as the obstruction. Gross offense against devotees is indeed a big road-blocker on devotional path. But even sincere practitioners may feel unoffensive and still experience dryness.


What most practitioners encounter instead is a subtler veil. Srila Jiva Gosvami identifies this veil not as active offense but as residual avidya—forgetfulness of our spiritual identity. This forgetfulness—avidya—manifesting as anarthas is the root of all offenses. Simply put, the Lord is fully present in every syllable of His Name, ready to accept loving service. Yet His response unfolds according to time, circumstance, and our inner readiness to recognize Him.


On the closing, we live in an age where almost everything, from instant orders to instant delivery to instant indulgence, is at the tap of an icon. In such a world, the patience required to allow the Lord to act in our lives can feel light-years away. That tension itself constitutes the test of faith, for the Lord does not always answer prayers in the manner we expect.


Sometimes the answer is yes.


Sometimes it is no.


Sometimes it is you must be kidding.


And other times—the answer is simply not yet.


Yet hearing God’s voice is no simple task. To truly hear Him, we need to let His voice descend into our range of perception. That means walking with faith, consistent with spiritual practices, being in the circle of sincere seekers; thus, allowing space for the Lord into our lives. This quiet resolve does not escape the Lord’s notice.


The Lord is eagerly on the lookout for those who love Him, inasmuch as He is willing to love them back. The Lord is on the lookout for those who intensely desire to see Him, inasmuch as He is willing to grace His presence. The Lord is on the lookout for sincere yet distracted seekers, inasmuch as He desires to take the role of a guide to bring them closer to Him.


In the Gaudiya Vaishnava tradition, the relationship between the seeker and the Divine is understood not as a one-sided pursuit, but as a gradual alignment of consciousness. Thus, divine grace is not coerced by effort nor triggered by emotional intensity alone; it becomes evident when self-centered expectation subside. In a sense, the Lord is said to be “captured” by devotion—not because He is overpowered; rather nothing remains that obscures His self-manifestation.


And the next time someone wonders, “When will you hear the Lord?” The answer is rarely instant: rather, it unfolds in its own graceful time.


Ashutosh Patnaik is a senior associate working in an MNC. He holds a master’s degree in finance. Besides work, he is an avid reader and writer in the genre of contemporary-cum-devotional subjects and a frequent contributor to BTG.
